# discovery-guide.md

Read this only when the mode is discovery. Everything here slots into the template in
`SKILL.md`; this file does not restate the shared spine, the guardrails or the question
gate.

This file owns section `[3] Walkthrough + probing`, and adds discovery-specific content
to `[2] Background` and `[4] Debrief + wrap`.

---

## When this mode applies

The question is about behaviour, context, workflow, needs or mental models: how people
do something today, what gets in their way, what they work around, who else is involved,
what would have to be true for something different to fit.

No artefact needs to exist. If the real question is whether an existing artefact is
usable, the mode is wrong. Go back to Step 3.

**Discovery has no tasks and no pass/fail.** Nothing is being completed, so there are no
task prompts, no completion measures and no post-task batteries. A discovery guide
containing any of those has drifted into the wrong mode.

## What Route A inherits

| From the plan | Becomes |
|---|---|
| Assumptions going in — the *evidence to look for* column | The probe branches. What would support or challenge an assumption is exactly what to probe toward |
| Lenses | The themes the sections are organised around |
| Participants → segments | The conditional branches: `[if they have used it before]`, `[if resistant]` |
| Session structure | Section timings, verbatim |
| Methodology → Stimulus | Whether anything is shown, and what it is for |
| The lens review point | When the guide may need revising — see *Revising an existing guide* in `SKILL.md` |

**The assumptions table is the highest-leverage source.** Its *evidence to look for*
column already names the behaviours that would support or challenge each belief, which
converts directly into what the walkthrough listens for.

**Never turn an assumption into a question.** The team's belief stays out of the room —
it is tested by what the participant does and says unprompted. A probe that names the
assumption has contaminated the only evidence that could have disconfirmed it.

## [2] Background

10% of the session — shorter than in usability, because the walkthrough that follows
does most of the context-setting.

Cover, briefly:

- Who they are and what they do, in their own words
- Where this activity sits in their work or life — how often, how much, who else is
  involved
- Any segmentation the study needs, where it cannot be established from screening

Keep it to orientation. The detail belongs in the walkthrough, anchored to a real
instance, not collected here as a general profile.

---

## [3] Walkthrough + probing

70% of the session, and the whole substance of the study. Nest by theme as `[3.1]`,
`[3.2]`.

Two movements, in order: establish what actually happened, then deepen it. Not two
separate sections — one flows into the other, and the probing follows whatever the
account surfaces.

### Opening the walkthrough

Anchor to a **specific recent instance**, never a general habit. This is the single most
important move in a discovery guide.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Weak** | *How do you usually handle this?* |
| **Sharp** | *Tell me about the last time you did this — walk me through it from the start.* |

Where the last instance was unrepresentative, ask for it anyway and then ask for a
typical one alongside. Two real accounts beat one generalisation.

Useful openers:

- *Walk me through how you [do this] currently — what do you use, and in what order?*
- *Tell me about the last time you [did this]. Start from what prompted it.*
- *What happened the most recent time this came up?*

### Following the account

- **Keep them in sequence.** *And then what happened? / What did you do next?* People
  summarise; the sequence is where the workarounds are.
- **Follow their detour.** Where the account goes somewhere unplanned, follow it. The
  guide is a frame, not a running order.
- **Ask about the other people.** Who else was involved, who they asked, who did it for
  them.
- **Note their words.** What they call things unprompted, especially where it differs
  from the product's terms. Vocabulary is evidence about mental models; asking someone
  to describe their mental model is not.
- **Absorbed friction is still friction.** A settled workaround counts even where the
  participant does not describe it as a problem.

### Infrequent behaviour and recall

Anchoring to the last instance only works where the last instance is recent enough to be
recalled properly. Where the behaviour happens rarely — twice a year, once per campaign —
the account thins out into a summary, which is the thing this mode exists to avoid.

- **Ask when it last happened before asking them to walk through it.** A vague answer is
  itself the signal that recall will be weak.
- **Use whatever they have to hand.** *Do you still have it? Can you show me what you
  did?* An artefact — a phone, a spreadsheet, an email thread, a notes app — reconstructs
  a sequence far better than memory.
- **Where recall is thin, say so in the notes** rather than accepting a tidy summary as
  an account.

### Show rather than tell, where possible

Participants describe what they think they do, which is not always what they do. Where
the behaviour can be demonstrated rather than recounted, ask to see it.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Weak** | *How do you keep track of them?* |
| **Sharp** | *Could you show me where they are on your phone now? Talk me through what you're doing as you look.* |

This is not usability testing — nothing is being evaluated. It is the same walkthrough,
using a real artefact instead of memory as the anchor.

### The Anticipated answer | Follow-ups device

The core instrument of a discovery guide. Where a question has a small set of likely
answers, pre-load the branch so the probing is not improvised live.

| Anticipated answer | Follow-ups |
|---|---|
| [A likely response] | WHAT is it about [this] specifically, and why? |
| [A likely response] | WHAT have they tried? What happened? |
| [A likely response] | WHY does that make it necessary? Where does the requirement come from? |

**Construction rules:**

- **Only where answers are genuinely predictable** — a known set of blockers, reasons,
  or categories. Where the answer is open, an opener and a probe ladder serve better.
- **Anticipated answers come from the user, the plan's assumptions, or prior work.**
  Never invent a plausible-sounding list.
- **Branches are prompts, not a script.** The participant may give an answer no branch
  covers; that is the study working.
- **Every branch asks why or how**, not whether. A branch that can be answered yes or no
  is not a branch.
- Where a branch exists to test a stated assumption, it probes toward the behaviour, not
  toward the belief.

Where the likely answers vary by segment rather than by content, use square-bracket
conditionals instead: `[if they have used it before]`, `[if resistant]`, `[if they do
this for someone else]`.

### Standard probes

A bank, drawn on as needed.

| Purpose | Probe |
|---|---|
| Goal | *What were you trying to get done? / What did you need at that point?* |
| Trigger | *What set that off? / How did you know it was time to do it?* |
| Sequence | *And then what? / What did you do next? / What happened before that?* |
| Cause | *What made you do it that way?* |
| Workaround | *Is that how you'd normally do it, or was that particular to this time?* |
| Consequence | *What happens when that goes wrong? / What did that mean for you?* |
| Trade-off | *Was there another way you could have done it? What made you pick this one?* |
| Other people | *Who else was involved? / Did anyone help with that?* |
| Vocabulary | *What do you call that? / Is that the word you'd use?* |
| Frequency, without claiming prevalence | *Does that happen often, or was that a one-off?* |
| Importance | *How much does that matter to you compared to the rest of it?* |

Never probe with a verdict attached. *"Was that annoying?"* fails every time.

### Where a stimulus is used

A stimulus is anything shown to prompt discussion — a current workflow, a competitor, a
sketch, a card sort. It is **not a design under test**.

- Establish the participant's own experience **first**, so their reaction can be read
  against that baseline.
- Take the stimulus time from the walkthrough, not from the probing.
- Treat reactions as evidence about their mental model, expectations or current
  behaviour — never as an evaluation of the stimulus.
- Say in the guide, in the moderator's own words, what the stimulus is for. It is easy
  to slide into usability testing the moment something is on screen.

## [4] Debrief + wrap

10% of the session.

- **What they would change** — asked as a condition, not a solution: *what would have to
  be different for this to work better for you?* See the *Probing* rules in `SKILL.md`.
- **Anything not covered** — *is there anything about this I haven't asked about that
  you think matters?* Discovery runs heavier on the unexpected than usability does, and
  this question is where a lot of it arrives.
- **Closing scales**, only where the study uses them.
- Thanks, and what happens next.

---

## Mini-quant in discovery

Discovery is qualitative. Scales are legitimate as **conversation prompts** or for
**segmentation**, and never as measures of prevalence or outcome.

Where one is used, state in the guide what it measures and what it is for. Anchor all
five points where the construct is self-rated and fuzzy — comfort with technology, for
instance, where a participant needs to know what a 3 means before they can pick one.

A scale in a discovery guide should open a conversation, not close one: ask the number,
then ask why they picked it.

## Timing

Percentages of the moderated session, from the plan's Session structure. Buffer is not
printed.

| Section | Share |
|---|---|
| `[1]` Welcome + intro | 5% |
| `[2]` Background | 10% |
| `[3]` Walkthrough + probing | 70% |
| `[4]` Debrief + wrap | 10% |

The printed sections sum to 95%; the remaining 5% is unprinted buffer. The 70% for `[3]`
combines the plan's current-behaviour and deepening allocations, which are one continuous
movement in the guide.

**Route A** — take the numbers from the plan's table directly, dropping buffer and any
break. Where the plan splits current behaviour and probing into two rows, print them as
one section unless the user wants them separate.

**Routes B and C** — apply the percentages to the stated session length, round each to
the nearest 5 minutes, give `[1]` a minimum of 5 minutes, and reconcile any difference
against `[3]`. Check the total.

Sessions run 30–60 minutes unless the user names a reason for longer.

If the session cannot hold the structure, cut a theme from `[3]` rather than compressing
every section. A walkthrough compressed to fit stops producing sequences.
